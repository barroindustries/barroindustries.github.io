Pandesal Oven Guide: Choosing and Running a Pugon for a Small Bakery

A pandesal oven has to bake early and in batches, so it sets the pace of the morning. This guide shows how to choose a Pugon, size it by trays and plan the heat, hood and gas around it.

What a pandesal oven has to do

Pandesal is a morning product. The dough is divided, shaped, rolled in crumbs and proofed before dawn, then baked tray after tray for opening. That asks three things of an oven:

  • Even heat. Every tray should brown the same, front to back.
  • Steady recovery. The third bake should come out like the first, so the oven must hold its temperature while you unload and reload.
  • Easy loading. A baker moves full trays in and out all morning, so the door matters as much as the burner.

A pugon can do this. For the whole line around it, see our commercial bakery equipment guide.

The pugon, old and new

A pugon is the traditional Filipino bakery oven: a brick or clay chamber heated by burning wood, with a flue to carry the smoke away.

Many bakeries now use a steel pugon, an insulated steel box heated by a burner instead of wood. The two differ in how you live with them:

Brick pugon and steel pugon compared
PointBrick pugon (wood)Steel pugon
Heat and controlWood burned in the chamber, controlled by the fire and the flue.A burner, so the flame is set at the control.
Smoke and ashSmoke, ash and firewood to handle.Heat and combustion gases.
SuitsBakeries that keep the traditional method.Pandesal and everyday bread.

Judge any pugon on how evenly it holds heat, how well it is insulated and how easily the baker can read the temperature. A wood fire also brings smoke and ash, so ask the Bureau of Fire Protection (BFP) and the city or municipal health office what applies to solid fuel on your site.

Choosing our Pugon

The Pugon is a steel oven built for pandesal and everyday bread. It is made to order, and these are its parts:

  • An SS304 interior and an insulated body, so heat stays in the oven and the room stays more bearable.
  • An H-type burner.
  • A counterweighted door that stays where you put it, so a baker with a full tray does not have to hold it open.
  • One deck (four trays) or two decks (eight trays).

The choice is the number of decks. One deck suits a small bakeshop that bakes in a few batches. Two decks hold eight trays at once, so the same morning needs fewer bakes. Sizing by trays and bakes

Work backwards from the morning, with your own numbers:

  • Count the pandesal on one tray.
  • Divide the pieces you need at opening by the pieces per tray. That is the trays for the morning.
  • Decide how many bakes the morning allows, leaving time to unload and reload.
  • Divide the trays by the bakes. That is what the oven must hold at once.

For example, with made-up numbers: 24 trays in three bakes means 8 trays at once, which is one two-deck Pugon. The same 24 trays in a one-deck Pugon, which holds four, would take six bakes, so check that your morning has the time.

Tell us your tray size, and use that one size for the oven, proofing cabinet, racks and work tables, so every tray fits.

Running the morning bake

An oven only performs as well as the morning around it. A few habits help:

  • Light and heat the oven before the first tray is ready, so the first bake starts at the temperature of the third.
  • Load trays in a steady rhythm. Every long pause at an open door lets heat out.
  • Stage the next tray from the proofing cabinet while the current one bakes, so the oven is never waiting for dough.

Write the time and temperature of a good bake on a card by the oven.

Heat, hood and gas

A gas oven for a bakery gives off heat and combustion gases, and the baker stands at its door in the early hours. Plan a hood and exhaust above the door and a fresh-air supply: our exhaust hood guide and fresh-air guide explain how. Keep the hot side away from the dough table and the dry store, and leave clear floor in front of the door.

A gas oven burns LPG, so list it with every other gas appliance and size the pipe for all of them running at once: see our LPG guide.

Ask the BFP and the city or municipal health office what applies to your bakery before the drawings are fixed.

Cleaning and care

Wipe crumbs and flour from the interior once the oven has cooled, because baked-on crumbs smoke at the next heat-up. Wipe the outer body with a soft cloth and mild soap. Keep flour off the door hinge and counterweight so the door keeps moving smoothly. Our preventive maintenance guide lists what to check and how often.

A checklist before you order

  • Pieces per tray, trays per bake and bakes before opening.
  • Your tray size, written on your request.
  • One deck or two.
  • Door and corridor widths at the narrowest point.
  • The hood route, the fresh-air supply and the LPG line.
  • A thermometer you can read from the front.

Frequently asked questions

What kind of oven do I need to bake pandesal?

Any oven that heats evenly and holds its temperature from bake to bake: a pugon, either wood-fired brick or steel. Size it by the trays you must bake before opening.

What is a pugon?

A pugon is the traditional Filipino bakery oven, classically a brick or clay chamber heated by wood and now often a steel box with a burner. Our steel Pugon has one deck of four trays or two decks of eight.

How do I work out how many trays my bakery needs?

Work backwards from opening time. Divide the pieces you need by the pieces on one tray, then divide the trays by the bakes the morning allows. The answer is what the oven must hold at once.

Does a gas oven for a bakery need a hood?

Yes. A gas oven gives off heat and combustion gases, so plan a hood, an exhaust and a fresh-air supply, and an LPG line sized for every gas appliance running at once. We plan them with the oven.

Do you make a pandesal oven or a pugon?

Yes. Our Pugon is a steel oven for pandesal and everyday bread, with an SS304 interior, an insulated body, an H-type burner and a counterweighted door, in one deck of four trays or two decks of eight.
